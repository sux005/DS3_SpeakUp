# SpeakUp: Filler-Word Rubric, Draft v3

Changes from v2: two rubric options (per 100 words, recommended, and per minute); more detail on the presentation studies.

## 1. Presentation studies

### Laske & DiGennaro Reed (2024): the threshold study

- **Citation:** *Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 57*(3), 574–583. University of Kansas. https://doi.org/10.1002/jaba.1093 (PubMed 38819033)
- **Design:** The researchers created speeches containing **filler sounds** (um, uh) and **filler words** at controlled rates of **0, 2, 5 and 12 per minute**. Raters recruited through a crowdsourcing research service judged the speaker's public-speaking performance across several categories.
- **Findings (from the published abstract):**
  - More disfluencies lowered ratings in most categories, and **filler sounds hurt more than filler words**.
  - The damage was clearest at **12 per minute**.
  - **5 per minute did not lower perceived effectiveness**; the authors conclude that **5 or fewer per minute may be acceptable**.
- **Why it matters for SpeakUp:** it's the only study found that tests specific filler *rates* on listener ratings of a speech, so it anchors the "acceptable" and "harmful" cutoffs. It also supports scoring um/uh first.
- **Caveats:** full text is paywalled; the exact filler words, speech length, rating scales and sample size are not in the abstract. Read before citing details.

### Niebuhr & Fischer (2019): real business presentations

- **Citation:** *Interspeech 2019*, 544–548. University of Southern Denmark. https://doi.org/10.21437/Interspeech.2019-1194 (full text read)
- **Speakers:** 68 managers (36 women, 32 men, ages 27–58) with under 5 years of public-speaking experience, recorded giving **2–5 minute presentations** at the start of a persuasive-communication course, **before any training**. Presentations were in German.
- **Stimuli:** one-minute excerpts from the middle of each presentation (openings and closings excluded).
- **Listeners:** 29 engineering students (ages 23–29), none with speaking training. 13 estimated how many fillers they heard while doing a distracting card-sorting task; 16 rated presentation performance from 0 ("extremely bad") to 10 ("absolutely excellent").
- **Findings:**
  - The managers' actual rate ranged from **1 to 13 filled pauses per minute, averaging 6.33**, which the authors note is typical for German speakers (430 filled pauses analyzed).
  - The **more fillers listeners noticed, the lower they rated the presentation** (r = −0.42).
  - **Longer fillers hurt more:** a 400 ms longer average "um" cost about 1 point on the 10-point scale, and the speakers with the longest fillers sounded about 50% less competent than those with the shortest (r = −0.31).
  - Short, nasal "mm"-like fillers were noticed less and penalized less.
- **Why it matters for SpeakUp:** it's a real presentation sample from untrained speakers, which is close to SpeakUp's users. Its 6.3 per minute average matches our live conference talks (6.2 per minute). It also suggests a future feature: measuring how *long* each "um" lasts, which word timestamps make possible.
- **Caveats:** German speakers; small listener groups; correlational.

### Schreiber, Paul & Shibley (2012): the college speaking rubric

- **Citation:** The development and test of the Public Speaking Competence Rubric. *Communication Education, 61*(3), 205–233. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ970753
- **What it is:** a rubric for grading college student speeches with **11 competencies** (9 core, 2 optional), each scored on a **0–4 scale: Deficient (0), Minimal (1), Basic (2), Proficient (3), Advanced (4)**, the same scale SpeakUp uses.
- **Fillers:** competency 7, vocal expression. The level descriptions say the advanced speaker avoids fillers; a lower-level speaker "generally avoids fillers (e.g., 'um,' 'uh,' 'like')"; for an ineffective speaker, fillers distract the audience.
- **Testing:**
  - Study 1: 5 trained faculty raters scored 45 speeches from an introductory college speech course. Rater agreement (intraclass correlation) ranged .54–.93 (informative) and .37–.88 (persuasive).
  - Study 2: 1 faculty member and 3 undergraduates scored 50 more speeches; agreement improved (.69–.95 and .46–.92). Scores predicted students' speech grades.
  - An independent ETS study (Joe et al. 2015) found vocal expression was one of the most reliably scored dimensions (ICC .75). In that study an expert gave a speaker 3 out of 4 on vocal expression while noting both "excellent vocal variety" and "uses 'um' excessively", which shows fillers are one part of delivery, not the whole score.
- **Why it matters for SpeakUp:** it shows college speech instructors treat um/uh (and filler "like") as a recognized part of delivery quality. It describes levels in words but gives **no numeric thresholds**; SpeakUp's rubric adds the numbers.

### College-student training studies (Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis, 2016–2022)

A series of 7 studies (reviewed by Luiselli 2022) treated university students' fillers during speeches with awareness and habit-reversal training:
- **Mancuso & Miltenberger (2016):** 6 participants; fillers defined as "uh," "um," "er," clicks, and misuse of "like"; all decreased immediately after training.
- **Spieler & Miltenberger (2017):** 4 university students; targeted filled pauses, tongue clicks and inappropriate "like"; awareness training reduced all of them, and participants reported better overall speaking.

**Why it matters for SpeakUp:** practicing with feedback reduces fillers, which is SpeakUp's whole loop. These abstracts don't report baseline rates, so they aren't used for cutoffs.

### Other supporting evidence

- **Brennan & Williams (1995):** answers preceded by "um" were judged as less certain. https://doi.org/10.1006/jmla.1995.1017
- **Christenfeld (1995):** listeners read "ums" as nervousness or lack of preparation. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02175503
- **Miller & Hewgill (1964):** more nonfluency in recorded speeches lowered perceived competence (as commonly summarized). https://doi.org/10.1080/00335636409382644
- **Counter-evidence:** Fraundorf & Watson (2011) found fillers can help listeners remember a story. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2011.03.004

## 2. What counts

**Definite fillers (scored, red):** um (umm, uhm), uh (uhh), er, erm, ah, and "you know" (except "do/did/don't/if/as/would/to you know").

**Investigate list (counted and shown, not scored):** filler "like", "I mean", so, well, actually, basically, right/okay, kind of/sort of, literally, hmm/mm, repeated words. Evidence for each is in section 5.

## 3. Option A (recommended): definite fillers per 100 words

**Formula:** (definite fillers ÷ spoken words) × 100. Spoken words exclude the fillers themselves, matching the WPM definition. "3 per 100 words" means 3% of words are fillers.

**Why recommended:** it measures how often fillers interrupt the *speech*, independent of how fast someone talks. Speed is already scored by WPM. In 100 conference talks, fillers per 100 words was nearly unrelated to WPM (r = 0.09), while fillers per minute was partly tied to it (r = 0.26). Example: 6 fillers per minute is 5.0 per 100 words for someone speaking at 120 WPM, but 3.5 for someone at 170 WPM. Per minute would score the faster speaker worse for the same filler habit.

| Score | Definite fillers per 100 words | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **4 Advanced** | under 2.0 | Fillers rare |
| **3 Proficient** | 2.0 to under 3.5 | Noticeable but within the range listeners accept |
| **2 Basic** | 3.5 to under 5.5 | Typical unscripted presenter |
| **1 Minimal** | 5.5 to under 8.0 | Frequent enough to distract |
| **0 Deficient** | 8.0 or more | Level shown to lower listener ratings |

(Level names borrowed from the Public Speaking Competence Rubric's 0–4 scale.)

**Benchmarks.** Per-minute research figures are converted at 145 WPM, the median speaking rate of the live conference talks and the center of SpeakUp's WPM rubric.

| Benchmark | Per 100 words | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Natural-science lecturers | ≈1.0 (1.39/min) | Schachter et al. (1991) |
| Tested level with no harm | ≈1.4 (2/min) | Laske & DiGennaro Reed (2024) |
| Social-science lecturers | ≈2.6 (3.84/min) | Schachter et al. (1991) |
| Highest level found acceptable | ≈3.4 (5/min) | Laske & DiGennaro Reed (2024) |
| Untrained managers' presentations | ≈4.4 (6.33/min) | Niebuhr & Fischer (2019) |
| Typical live conference talk | 4.5 (middle half 2.7–5.8) | Our analysis, 50 ACL 2019 talks |
| Level that lowered ratings | ≈8.3 (12/min) | Laske & DiGennaro Reed (2024) |

**How the cutoffs line up:** under 2.0 earns a 4 (around fluent lecturers and Laske's 2-per-minute level); the 3.5 boundary is Laske's "acceptable" level; 8.0 is Laske's "harmful" level; the typical untrained or unscripted presenter (≈4.5) is a 2, with "under 3.5" as a concrete next goal.

**Spread on live conference talks:** 4 = 22%, 3 = 14%, 2 = 30%, 1 = 22%, 0 = 12%.

## 4. Option B: definite fillers per minute

**Formula:** definite fillers ÷ minutes from first to last word.

**Pros:** matches the units of the research directly (Laske, Schachter, Niebuhr); easy to picture ("6 ums a minute"); matches the project doc's session table. **Con:** partly tied to speaking speed (r = 0.26), so fast speakers score slightly worse for the same habit.

| Score | Definite fillers per minute | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **4 Advanced** | under 3 | Fillers rare |
| **3 Proficient** | 3 to under 5 | Within the range listeners accept |
| **2 Basic** | 5 to under 8 | Typical unscripted presenter |
| **1 Minimal** | 8 to under 12 | Frequent enough to distract |
| **0 Deficient** | 12 or more | Level shown to lower listener ratings |

**Benchmarks:** natural-science lecturers 1.39, social-science 3.84, humanities 4.85 (Schachter 1991); no harm at 2 and 5, harm at 12 (Laske 2024); untrained managers 6.33 (Niebuhr & Fischer 2019); live conference talks 6.2, middle half 3.9–9.4 (our data).

**Spread on live conference talks:** 4 = 22%, 3 = 10%, 2 = 36%, 1 = 20%, 0 = 12%.

**Option A vs. B:** the same score for 85% of the 100 talks, never more than 1 point apart. Option A's cutoffs are Option B's divided by 1.45 (the 145-WPM conversion), rounded.

## 5. Investigate list: evidence so far

| Word | Evidence | Open question | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| like (filler use) | Named in the college speaking rubric; targeted ("misuse of like") in the student training studies; 22% of "like"s are fillers in Switchboard; classifier totals within 6% but individual highlights ~65% right | Detection accuracy on presentations | Hand-label "like" in student clips; promote when precision ≥ 90% |
| I mean | 94% filler/editing term in Switchboard | Often starts a real correction | Check correction vs. filler in presentations |
| so | On Toastmasters' list; most common candidate (2,412 in 100 ACL talks) | Only 8% filler in Switchboard | Flag only extreme sentence-initial overuse |
| well | On Toastmasters' list; 90% discourse marker in Switchboard | Often an adverb in presentations ("works well") | Needs a classifier |
| actually / basically | On the project doc's list; 30% / 1% filler in Switchboard | Mostly meaningful | Hand-label a sample; likely drop "basically" |
| right? / okay | 0.3% / 1.5% in Switchboard | Audience check-ins vs. tics | Look at position (end of sentence) |
| kind of / sort of | Hedges | Whether audiences penalize hedging | Literature review |
| literally, hmm, mm | Rare | — | Low priority |
| repeated words | Counted in disfluency research (Bortfeld 2001; Miller & Hewgill 1964) | Better as a separate "repetitions" count | Verbatim Whisper keeps them |

## 6. Measurement notes

- **Transcriber must keep fillers:** on 436 human-labeled um/uh, plain Whisper caught 13%, verbatim Whisper 83%. Test Amazon Transcribe the same way.
- Verbatim Whisper misses ~17% of fillers, so scores run slightly generous; acceptable for a practice tool, recheck with hand-labeled clips.
- Benchmarks come from lecturers, managers, researchers and listener experiments, not UCSD students; collect student baselines from the team's consented recordings.
